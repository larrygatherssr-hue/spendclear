Debt Snowball vs Avalanche: Which Payoff Method Is Right for You?

If you have more than one debt, you have probably wondered which one to pay off first. Two popular methods answer that question in different ways: the snowball and the avalanche. Both work. Both have helped many people become debt-free. The difference is what each method prioritizes — quick wins or lower total cost.

This guide explains both methods in plain language, shows how each one works with a real example, and helps you choose the one that fits you best.

What Is the Debt Snowball Method?

The snowball method focuses on your smallest balance first. You keep paying the minimum on every debt, and you put any extra money toward the debt with the smallest balance, no matter its interest rate. When that debt is paid off, you take the money you were paying on it and add it to the next smallest debt. Like a snowball rolling downhill, your payments grow bigger as each debt disappears.

Here is why people like it:

- **Quick wins.** Paying off a small debt early feels good and builds confidence.

- **Fewer bills to juggle.** Each payoff means one less payment to track.

- **Motivation.** Seeing debts disappear keeps you going through a long payoff journey.

The snowball method is about behavior and momentum. If you need encouragement to keep going, early wins can make a real difference.

What Is the Debt Avalanche Method?

The avalanche method focuses on the highest interest rate first. You keep paying the minimum on every debt, and you put any extra money toward the debt with the highest interest rate, no matter its balance. When that debt is paid off, you move to the next highest rate.

Here is why people like it:

- **Lower total cost.** Paying off high-rate debt first usually means less interest paid over time.

- **Faster payoff overall.** Because less money goes to interest, more goes to reducing balances.

- **Logical order.** The most expensive debt goes first, which makes sense on paper.

The avalanche method is about math. If you are motivated by saving money and you can stay patient while a larger balance takes longer to clear, it can be the better choice.

A Worked Example (Made-Up Numbers)

Let us compare both methods with the same made-up round numbers. Imagine Jordan has three debts and can pay $400 a month total toward debt:

- **Card A:** $600 balance, minimum payment $25 a month

- **Card B:** $1,500 balance, minimum payment $50 a month

- **Loan C:** $3,000 balance, minimum payment $125 a month

The minimums add up to $200, so Jordan has $200 extra each month.

**With the snowball method**, Jordan targets Card A first because it has the smallest balance. Card A gets $225 a month ($25 minimum + $200 extra). In about three months, Card A is paid off. Then Jordan adds that $225 to Card B's payment, so Card B now gets $275 a month. When Card B is gone, the full $400 goes to Loan C. The debts fall one by one, and Jordan gets an early win in the first few months.

**With the avalanche method**, the order depends on interest rates. Let us say Card B has the highest rate, then Card A, then Loan C. Jordan puts the extra $200 toward Card B first, so Card B gets $250 a month. It takes longer to get the first payoff, because Card B is bigger than Card A. But over the whole journey, Jordan pays less interest, because the most expensive debt was attacked first.

Both methods end with all three debts paid off. The snowball gives a faster first win. The avalanche usually costs less overall. This is only an example with made-up numbers. Your debts and rates will be different, but the way each method works stays the same.

Which Method Keeps You Motivated?

This is where the snowball often wins. Research on behavior and habit has long shown that small wins build momentum. Paying off a $500 store card in a few months feels like real progress. It proves the plan works, and it gives you energy for the next debt.

The avalanche can feel slow at the start, especially if your highest-rate debt also has a large balance. Months can pass before the first debt disappears. If you are patient and motivated by the numbers, that is fine. If you need to see progress to stay on track, the snowball may suit you better.

Ask yourself honestly: what keeps me going? If the answer is quick wins, choose the snowball. If the answer is knowing you are paying the least interest possible, choose the avalanche.

How to Set Up Your Plan

Whichever method you choose, the setup is the same:

1. **List every debt.** Write down the balance, interest rate, and minimum payment for each one.

2. **Rank them.** For the snowball, rank by balance from smallest to largest. For the avalanche, rank by interest rate from highest to lowest.

3. **Pay minimums on all of them.** This keeps every account in good standing.

4. **Put all extra money on the top-ranked debt.** Every extra dollar goes to that one debt until it is gone.

5. **Roll the payment forward.** When a debt is paid off, add its payment to the next debt on your list.

6. **Review monthly.** Check your balances, celebrate payoffs, and adjust if your income or costs change.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, known as the CFPB, has free worksheets that can help you list your debts and build a payoff order.

Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Which method is better, snowball or avalanche?**

A: It depends on what keeps you going. The avalanche usually costs less in interest, while the snowball often keeps people motivated with early wins. The best method is the one you will stick with until the debts are gone.

**Q: Can I switch methods halfway through?**

A: Yes. Many people start with the snowball for a quick win and then switch to the avalanche. The important thing is to have a clear order at any given time, rather than paying extra randomly across debts.

**Q: What if my highest-rate debt is also my smallest debt?**

A: Then both methods point to the same debt first, which makes the choice easy. That happens more often than people expect, and it is a great place to start.
